Four tonalities to know this term
TonalityHow it is builtHow it tends to feelListen for
PentatonicFive notes, no semitone clashes (for example C D E G A).Open, folk-like, easy on the ear.Tunes that sound like they fit the black keys.
MajorDiatonic major scale (tone–tone–semitone pattern).Bright, settled.A confident resolution to the tonic.
MinorDiatonic minor scale.Darker, more serious.A lowered third compared with major.
AeolianThe natural minor mode (white notes A to A).Minor but plain, no raised leading note.A minor feel with a flat seventh.
Tell major from minor first

Start by deciding bright or dark. The clue is the third degree of the scale. A major third sounds bright and open; a minor third sounds darker. Once you hear minor, check the seventh: if the note below the tonic is a whole tone away, you are likely hearing Aeolian rather than harmonic minor.

In class this fortnight
  • 01Play C major, then C minor on keyboard. Name the note that changed (the third).
  • 02Play the black keys only to hear a pentatonic scale.
  • 03Play A to A on white keys to hear Aeolian.
  • 04Sing each scale up and down, then identify a teacher-played example by ear.
Success criteria
  • Hear major versus minor reliably.
  • Identify pentatonic and Aeolian.
  • Say what you heard that told you.
Where this fits

The first step of the Year 11 aural progression. Dorian, Lydian and Mixolydian come later, in Creative practice.
