Lesson 33: Unit launch: how screen music works

Lesson 33 of 50 · Listening, analysing and responding

Learning intention

Define diegetic and non-diegetic music and tell them apart by ear. Explain the cue, spotting and sync points using two contrasting scenes, and understand the term’s structure: taught core, individual study, examination.

By the end you can: Classify music in a scene as diegetic or non-diegetic and point to one spotting or sync decision the composer made.

Outcomes MU1-11L-01

Success criteria

Tick them off as you go.

In classThis fortnight

  1. Watch two contrasting scenes: one with clearly diegetic music (a source visible or implied in the world of the scene), one with clearly non-diegetic music.
  2. Identify the cue’s start and stop points in each scene and discuss why the composer chose those moments (spotting).
  3. Find at least one sync point in each scene, where music and image line up precisely.
  4. Well-formed dot points: diegetic or non-diegetic, and one spotting or sync decision, for each scene.

CompareScreen music vocabulary

TermMeaningA question to ask of a scene
DiegeticMusic the characters in the scene can hear.Is there a visible or implied source, like a radio or a band?
Non-diegeticMusic the audience hears but the characters do not.Is there no source in the world of the scene at all?
CueA discrete piece of music written for a specific scene or moment.Where does this piece of music start and stop?
SpottingThe process of deciding where music starts and stops in a film.Why does the music begin exactly here, not earlier or later?
Sync pointA moment where music and image line up precisely.Is there a hit, a cut or a gesture the music lands on exactly?

LearnWhy this matters

Screen music is written to do a job for the image, which makes it a different kind of listening from concert music. Every choice, from whether music plays at all to where it starts and stops, is a decision about what the audience should feel or understand at that exact moment.
